================================================================================
PART 1 — SINE WAVES IN TRADING
================================================================================

1.1 THE IDEA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The premise is that price contains repeating cycles: a security oscillates
around some trend at a roughly regular period, so if you can measure the period
and the phase, you can anticipate turns instead of reacting to them.

Mathematically you are asserting that price can be usefully written as

    price(t) = trend(t) + A * sin(2*pi*t/T + phase) + noise(t)

where T is the cycle length, A the amplitude, and phase tells you where in the
cycle you currently sit. Everything in cycle analysis is an attempt to estimate
T, A, and phase from a finite, noisy, non-stationary sample.

That last phrase is the whole problem. Markets are non-stationary: T and A drift
over time, and the "noise" is not well-behaved. A technique that recovers a clean
sine from a radio signal will happily recover an equally clean, equally
meaningless sine from a random walk.


1.2 BOUNDED OSCILLATORS (RSI, STOCHASTICS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
These are the practical, everyday descendants of cycle thinking. They do not
claim to find a period; they rescale recent price action into a fixed range so
that "high" and "low" have consistent meaning across securities.

RSI (Relative Strength Index), Wilder, 14 periods by default:

    RS  = average gain over N periods / average loss over N periods
    RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + RS)

Bounded 0-100 by construction. Traditional reading: >70 overbought, <30
oversold. That traditional reading is also where most people go wrong, because
in a strong trend RSI parks above 70 for weeks and every "overbought" sale is a
mistake.

The screener uses RSI differently, and deliberately: it scores the 50-70 band
highest (screener/score.py, momentum_score). The reasoning is that 50 is where
control changes hands between buyers and sellers, so 50-70 means an uptrend that
is established but not yet stretched. Above 75 the screener flags "Overbought"
rather than rewarding it. This is a momentum-continuation reading, not a
mean-reversion one, and it suits a one-month horizon.

Stochastics (Lane) ask a different question — where does the close sit inside
the recent range?

    %K = 100 * (close - lowest_low_N) / (highest_high_N - lowest_low_N)
    %D = moving average of %K (typically 3 periods)

%K is jumpy; %D smooths it; crossovers are the signal. Stochastics are more
responsive than RSI and correspondingly noisier. In a range-bound market they
are genuinely useful. In a trend they whipsaw.

Both are bounded oscillators: they compress an unbounded quantity (price) into a
fixed interval. That compression is what makes them comparable across a
17,000-stock universe, and it is also what makes them lossy. A stock at RSI 65
and $1,043 (MU) and a stock at RSI 65 and $1.20 are not equivalent situations,
but the oscillator says they are.


1.3 DETRENDED PRICE OSCILLATOR (DPO)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
If you want to see cycles, the trend is in the way — it dominates the variance
and hides everything else. DPO removes it:

    DPO = price(t - (N/2 + 1)) - SMA_N(t)

The shift is the point. By comparing a past price to a centred moving average,
DPO strips the dominant trend and leaves shorter oscillation. It is explicitly
NOT a momentum indicator and is not meant to be used for current-bar signals —
the lookback shift means the most recent values are not defined the way traders
often assume.

What DPO is good for: measuring the typical distance between peaks, i.e.
estimating cycle length T empirically. What it is bad for: timing entries, since
by construction it is looking backwards by half a cycle.


1.4 HURST CYCLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
J.M. Hurst's framework (1970s) proposes that price is the sum of several nested
cycles of different lengths, roughly harmonically related — a long cycle
containing shorter ones, each about half the length of the one above. The
practical claims are:

  - Summation: observed price is the sum of these components plus trend.
  - Harmonicity: periods relate by small integer ratios (often 2:1).
  - Synchronicity: troughs of different cycles tend to align.
  - Proportionality: longer cycles have larger amplitude.

Traders using this framework look for nested troughs lining up and treat those
alignments as high-probability turning points.

The honest assessment: the framework is internally coherent and produces
beautiful charts in hindsight. Its weak point is that with enough nested
components of adjustable length and phase, you can fit any series whatsoever.
The fit is not evidence.

Separately, note that "Hurst cycles" (J.M. Hurst, trader) and the "Hurst
exponent" (H.E. Hurst, hydrologist) are different things by different people.
The Hurst exponent H measures long-memory in a series: H = 0.5 is a random walk,
H > 0.5 trending/persistent, H < 0.5 mean-reverting. The exponent is a
legitimate statistical measure with real standard errors. It is also noisy on
short financial samples and drifts over time. Do not conflate the two.


1.5 WHERE SEASONALITY ENDS AND CURVE-FITTING BEGINS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the part that matters, so it gets its own section.

There ARE real calendar effects with plausible mechanisms:

  - Earnings cycles. Quarterly reporting is a genuine 63-trading-day rhythm
    imposed by regulation. Post-earnings-announcement drift is one of the most
    replicated anomalies in the literature.
  - Tax-loss selling in December and the January reversal that follows.
  - Index rebalancing on scheduled dates, forcing mechanical flows.
  - Dividend and ex-dividend dates.
  - Sector seasonality with a physical cause: natural gas demand in winter,
    retail in Q4, agricultural cycles.
  - Option expiry and quarterly triple-witching.

What these share is a MECHANISM you can state in one sentence without using the
word "cycle." That is the test.

A pattern is probably curve-fitting when:

  - The period was discovered by searching many periods and keeping the best.
    If you test 100 cycle lengths at the 5% level, five will look significant by
    construction. Almost nobody reports how many they tested.
  - The cycle length needs re-tuning every few months to keep working. A real
    period does not drift to fit the data.
  - It only works on one instrument and vanishes on similar ones.
  - It only works on the sample where it was found. Out-of-sample decay from
    "significant" to "nothing" is the norm for discovered cycles.
  - The explanation invokes market psychology in general terms rather than a
    specific, datable, repeating event.
  - Removing a handful of observations destroys it.

The asymmetry worth internalising: a real cycle needs a calendar or an
institutional forcing function. "Prices tend to oscillate" is not a mechanism —
it is a description of any mean-reverting noise process, including one you
generated with a random number generator.

Relevant to this project: the screener uses no cycle detection at all. RSI
appears only as a momentum band (50-70 good, >75 flagged), and the one-month
horizon is justified by post-upgrade analyst drift — a mechanism with a date
attached — not by any wave-fitting. That was a deliberate choice.


================================================================================
PART 2 — ETFs
================================================================================

2.1 STRUCTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
An ETF is a fund whose shares trade on an exchange. The mechanism that makes it
work is creation/redemption: Authorised Participants (large institutions) can
exchange a basket of the underlying securities for a block of ETF shares
("creation unit", typically 25,000-50,000 shares) and vice versa.

That mechanism is an arbitrage loop. If the ETF trades above the value of its
holdings (NAV), an AP buys the basket, creates shares, and sells them, pushing
the price back down. This is why ETF prices track NAV closely, and why they can
break: in a stressed market, if the underlying stops trading or becomes
impossible to price, the arbitrage loop stalls and the ETF can trade at a
meaningful discount or premium. That happened in corporate bond ETFs in March
2020.

Legal structures differ and it matters for taxes and mechanics:
  - Open-end funds (most equity ETFs) — can reinvest dividends, lend securities.
  - Unit Investment Trusts (SPY, QQQ historically) — cannot reinvest dividends,
    which creates a small cash drag.
  - Grantor trusts (commodity funds like GLD) — hold the physical asset.
  - ETNs — unsecured debt of the issuer, NOT a fund. You carry issuer credit
    risk. If the bank fails, your ETN is a claim in bankruptcy.


2.2 EXPENSE RATIOS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Quoted annually, accrued daily out of NAV. You never see a bill; the fund just
shrinks slightly relative to its index.

Typical ranges as of 2026:
  - Broad market index (VOO, VTI, SPY):        0.03% - 0.09%
  - Sector and factor funds:                   0.10% - 0.50%
  - Actively managed:                          0.35% - 1.00%
  - Leveraged / inverse:                       0.85% - 1.10%
  - Niche thematic:                            0.50% - 0.95%

For a one-month hold, the expense ratio is nearly irrelevant: 0.95% annual is
about 0.08% over a month, which is smaller than a typical bid-ask spread on a
thin fund. Expense ratios matter for years-long holds; spreads matter for
short ones. Optimising the wrong one is a common beginner error.


2.3 LIQUIDITY AND SPREADS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The counter-intuitive fact: an ETF's tradable liquidity is not its own volume.
It is the liquidity of what it holds, because APs can always create more shares
from the underlying basket.

A fund with 20,000 shares/day of screen volume that holds S&P 500 constituents
can absorb a large order at a fair price. A fund with the same screen volume
holding illiquid small-cap foreign equities cannot.

What actually drives the spread you pay:
  - Liquidity of the underlying basket (dominant factor).
  - Whether the underlying market is open. An ETF on Japanese equities trading
    at 2pm New York has a stale basket; market makers widen spreads to cover the
    risk, which is why international ETF spreads blow out outside local hours.
  - Volatility — spreads widen with uncertainty.
  - Time of day. The open and the close are the worst. The first and last 15
    minutes routinely carry spreads several times the midday level.
  - Competition among market makers.

Practical rules: use limit orders, never market orders, on anything but the most
liquid funds. Avoid the first and last 15 minutes. Compare the quote to the
fund's iNAV where published. For a fund quoting 0.30% spread, a round trip costs
0.60% before you are right about anything — on a one-month trade that is a
meaningful hurdle.


2.4 LEVERAGED AND INVERSE DECAY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the single most misunderstood product category in retail investing, so
here is the arithmetic.

A 3x ETF promises 3x the DAILY return, and it rebalances its exposure every day
to maintain that ratio. Compounding daily returns is not the same as multiplying
a period return by three.

Worked example. Index at 100, 3x fund at 100.
  Day 1: index +10% -> 110.   Fund +30% -> 130.
  Day 2: index -9.09% -> 100. Fund -27.27% -> 94.55.

The index is exactly flat. The fund lost 5.45%. Nobody made a mistake; no fee
caused it. It is the geometry of compounding, and it is called volatility decay
or beta slippage.

The loss scales roughly with the square of volatility and linearly with time:

    drag ≈ (L^2 - L) / 2 * sigma^2 * t

where L is leverage and sigma is volatility. At 3x leverage in a 40% volatility
environment, that is roughly 48% annualised drag against you in a flat market.

Consequences:
  - In a choppy sideways market, leveraged funds bleed in both directions.
    Both a 3x long and a 3x short on the same index can lose money in the same
    month. This is normal, not a malfunction.
  - In a smooth sustained trend they can OUTPERFORM their multiple, because
    daily rebalancing compounds gains.
  - Holding periods should be days, not months. The prospectuses say this
    explicitly and are ignored.
  - Inverse funds have the same problem plus a structural headwind, since
    equities drift up over long horizons.

For the one-month horizon this screener targets, leveraged ETFs are a poor fit.


2.5 HOW ETFs DIFFER FROM SINGLE STOCKS FOR SHORT-TERM TRADING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - No idiosyncratic catalyst. A diversified ETF cannot gap 30% on a trial
    result or a buyout. The fat right tail that makes single-stock momentum
    attractive is diversified away, along with the fat left tail.
  - No analyst coverage. Nobody issues a price target on SPY. This is why the
    screener's entire selection mechanism — consensus ratings, upgrades,
    target upside — is inapplicable to ETFs. The screener found roughly 2,500
    NYSEARCA and BATS listings in the 18,337-stock universe, and essentially
    none of them can pass an analyst-based filter, because the data does not
    exist.
  - Earnings dates do not apply, though the underlying holdings' earnings do
    move the fund.
  - Lower volatility at equal notional, from diversification.
  - Tracking error and roll costs in commodity and volatility products can
    dominate the thesis. VIX futures ETFs in contango lose to roll regardless
    of direction.
  - Tax treatment varies by structure (commodities, currencies, and ETNs all
    differ from equity funds).

Implication for this project: ETFs need a completely separate scoring model
based on flows, momentum, spread, and tracking quality. Scoring them with the
current analyst-driven model would produce nonsense, which is exactly why
`explore` treats them as their own category and ranks them on price/technical
signals only.


================================================================================
PART 3 — PENNY STOCKS
================================================================================

3.1 OTC VERSUS LISTED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
"Penny stock" is about price (SEC: under $5), not venue. The venue is a separate
and more important axis.

LISTED (NYSE, NASDAQ, NYSE American):
  - Must meet initial and continued listing standards: minimum price, market
    cap, shareholder count, corporate governance.
  - Full SEC reporting.
  - Sub-$1 for 30 consecutive days triggers a deficiency notice; failure to cure
    means delisting to OTC. This is why you see reverse splits — they are often
    a listing-compliance manoeuvre, not a vote of confidence.

OTC — tiered, and the tier tells you most of what you need:
  - OTCQX     — highest tier, audited financials, no shells, no sub-penny.
  - OTCQB     — venture tier, $0.01 minimum bid, annual verification.
  - Pink      — subdivided by disclosure: Current, Limited, or No Information.
                "Pink No Information" means the company tells you nothing.
  - Expert Market — broker-dealers cannot publicly quote these; retail
                effectively cannot buy. Often where failed companies end up.

In the screener's universe, OTCMKTS was the single largest exchange bucket
(~2,500 listings). Almost none of it is investable by the criteria in this
project.


3.2 FLOAT AND DILUTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Float = shares actually available to trade, i.e. shares outstanding minus
insider and restricted holdings. Small float means violent moves on modest
volume, in both directions.

The structural problem with the sub-$1 world is dilution, and the common
mechanism is the toxic convertible:

  - Company needs cash and cannot raise conventionally.
  - It issues a convertible note to a specialist financier.
  - The note converts at a DISCOUNT to market price — often 20-40% below
    whatever the price is at conversion time.
  - The financier converts, immediately sells into the market, price falls.
  - The lower price means the next conversion yields MORE shares.
  - Repeat.

This is a death spiral, and it is self-reinforcing by design. The share count
can go from 50 million to several billion inside a year. Every technical signal
on the chart is noise next to a financing structure that mechanically issues
shares into any rally.

How to check before believing any chart: read the share count trend across the
last several 10-Qs. If shares outstanding are compounding, nothing else matters.
Also check for a recent reverse split — a 1-for-100 reverse split followed by a
price collapse back to the old level is the signature of a company that has done
this repeatedly.


3.3 LIQUIDITY TRAPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The recurring pattern: entering is easy, exiting is not.

  - Spreads. A stock quoted 0.0021 x 0.0025 carries a ~19% spread. You are
    down 19% the instant you fill, and you need a 23% move just to break even.
  - Displayed size is tiny. The bid might be 5,000 shares. Your 500,000-share
    position cannot exit at the quote; you walk the book down.
  - Market orders are dangerous — you can fill far from the last print.
  - Halts. The SEC can suspend trading for two weeks on public-interest
    grounds, usually after promotion. You cannot exit a halted stock at any
    price, and reopening prints are frequently catastrophic.
  - Pump-and-dump. Newsletter and social promotion, price spikes on volume,
    promoters exit into retail buying, price round-trips. The volume that
    attracts a screener is the same volume created by the promotion.
  - Sub-penny tick behaviour makes a "1 cent move" a 50% move.

The screener's $1,000,000 average-daily-dollar-volume filter exists exactly to
exclude this. That single threshold removes most of the OTC universe. The
`explore` penny-stock mode deliberately relaxes the price floor but keeps a
volume floor, and labels the result as higher risk, because the alternative is
producing a list of untradeable names.


3.4 WHY ANALYST COVERAGE IS NEARLY ABSENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The screener's own data makes this concrete: of 18,337 stocks pulled,
approximately 11,324 had NO consensus rating at all. That is about 62% of the
universe with zero analyst coverage.

The economics explain it entirely:

  - Research is expensive. An analyst covering a name properly costs a firm
    low-to-mid six figures a year, all-in.
  - Research is paid for indirectly — through trading commissions, investment
    banking relationships, or (post-MiFID II in Europe) explicit payment.
  - A company with a $20 million market cap cannot generate enough institutional
    commission flow to fund coverage. Institutions cannot buy a meaningful
    position without moving the price, so they do not participate, so there is
    no commission.
  - No banking fees either — these companies do not do large secondaries or
    acquisitions through bulge-bracket banks.
  - Compliance risk. Covering a company with thin disclosure and unreliable
    financials creates liability for the research firm.
  - Index exclusion. Not being in an index means no passive flow and no
    benchmark-driven institutional interest.

Where "coverage" of microcaps does exist, it is frequently ISSUER-PAID research:
the company pays a firm to publish. Disclosure is legally required but buried.
Treat a lone "Strong Buy" on a $30 million company as marketing until proven
otherwise.

This is the honest reason the screener's core method cannot be pointed at penny
stocks. The entire scoring model rests on analyst consensus, upgrade drift, and
price targets. In a universe with no analysts, there is nothing to score. The
`explore` penny mode therefore falls back to price and volume signals only, and
says so.

PART 4 — HOW THIS CONNECTS TO THE SCREENER
================================================================================

  - RSI 50-70 scoring (score.py: momentum_score) is the only oscillator concept
    used. No cycle detection, no wave fitting, by choice.
  - The one-month horizon is justified by analyst-upgrade drift, which has a
    date-stamped mechanism, not by any cyclical claim.
  - The $1M dollar-volume floor and the 3-analyst minimum are what keep the
    main screen out of the penny-stock and no-coverage swamp.
  - ETFs and penny stocks are separated into their own `explore` categories
    precisely because the analyst-based model does not transfer to them, and
    pretending otherwise would generate confident nonsense.
