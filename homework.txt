================================================================================
PART 1 — SINE WAVES IN TRADING
================================================================================

1.1 THE IDEA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The premise is that price contains repeating cycles: a security oscillates
around some trend at a roughly regular period, so if you can measure the period
and the phase, you can anticipate turns instead of reacting to them.

Mathematically you are asserting that price can be usefully written as

    price(t) = trend(t) + A * sin(2*pi*t/T + phase) + noise(t)

where T is the cycle length, A the amplitude, and phase tells you where in the
cycle you currently sit. Everything in cycle analysis is an attempt to estimate
T, A, and phase from a finite, noisy, non-stationary sample.

That last phrase is the whole problem. Markets are non-stationary: T and A drift
over time, and the "noise" is not well-behaved. A technique that recovers a clean
sine from a radio signal will happily recover an equally clean, equally
meaningless sine from a random walk.


1.2 BOUNDED OSCILLATORS (RSI, STOCHASTICS)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
These are the practical, everyday descendants of cycle thinking. They do not
claim to find a period; they rescale recent price action into a fixed range so
that "high" and "low" have consistent meaning across securities.

RSI (Relative Strength Index), Wilder, 14 periods by default:

    RS  = average gain over N periods / average loss over N periods
    RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + RS)

Bounded 0-100 by construction. Traditional reading: >70 overbought, <30
oversold. That traditional reading is also where most people go wrong, because
in a strong trend RSI parks above 70 for weeks and every "overbought" sale is a
mistake.

The screener uses RSI differently, and deliberately: it scores the 50-70 band
highest (screener/score.py, momentum_score). The reasoning is that 50 is where
control changes hands between buyers and sellers, so 50-70 means an uptrend that
is established but not yet stretched. Above 75 the screener flags "Overbought"
rather than rewarding it. This is a momentum-continuation reading, not a
mean-reversion one, and it suits a one-month horizon.

Stochastics (Lane) ask a different question — where does the close sit inside
the recent range?

    %K = 100 * (close - lowest_low_N) / (highest_high_N - lowest_low_N)
    %D = moving average of %K (typically 3 periods)

%K is jumpy; %D smooths it; crossovers are the signal. Stochastics are more
responsive than RSI and correspondingly noisier. In a range-bound market they
are genuinely useful. In a trend they whipsaw.

Both are bounded oscillators: they compress an unbounded quantity (price) into a
fixed interval. That compression is what makes them comparable across a
17,000-stock universe, and it is also what makes them lossy. A stock at RSI 65
and $1,043 (MU) and a stock at RSI 65 and $1.20 are not equivalent situations,
but the oscillator says they are.


1.3 DETRENDED PRICE OSCILLATOR (DPO)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
If you want to see cycles, the trend is in the way — it dominates the variance
and hides everything else. DPO removes it:

    DPO = price(t - (N/2 + 1)) - SMA_N(t)

The shift is the point. By comparing a past price to a centred moving average,
DPO strips the dominant trend and leaves shorter oscillation. It is explicitly
NOT a momentum indicator and is not meant to be used for current-bar signals —
the lookback shift means the most recent values are not defined the way traders
often assume.

What DPO is good for: measuring the typical distance between peaks, i.e.
estimating cycle length T empirically. What it is bad for: timing entries, since
by construction it is looking backwards by half a cycle.


1.4 HURST CYCLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
J.M. Hurst's framework (1970s) proposes that price is the sum of several nested
cycles of different lengths, roughly harmonically related — a long cycle
containing shorter ones, each about half the length of the one above. The
practical claims are:

  - Summation: observed price is the sum of these components plus trend.
  - Harmonicity: periods relate by small integer ratios (often 2:1).
  - Synchronicity: troughs of different cycles tend to align.
  - Proportionality: longer cycles have larger amplitude.

Traders using this framework look for nested troughs lining up and treat those
alignments as high-probability turning points.

The honest assessment: the framework is internally coherent and produces
beautiful charts in hindsight. Its weak point is that with enough nested
components of adjustable length and phase, you can fit any series whatsoever.
The fit is not evidence.

Separately, note that "Hurst cycles" (J.M. Hurst, trader) and the "Hurst
exponent" (H.E. Hurst, hydrologist) are different things by different people.
The Hurst exponent H measures long-memory in a series: H = 0.5 is a random walk,
H > 0.5 trending/persistent, H < 0.5 mean-reverting. The exponent is a
legitimate statistical measure with real standard errors. It is also noisy on
short financial samples and drifts over time. Do not conflate the two.


1.5 WHERE SEASONALITY ENDS AND CURVE-FITTING BEGINS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the part that matters, so it gets its own section.

There ARE real calendar effects with plausible mechanisms:

  - Earnings cycles. Quarterly reporting is a genuine 63-trading-day rhythm
    imposed by regulation. Post-earnings-announcement drift is one of the most
    replicated anomalies in the literature.
  - Tax-loss selling in December and the January reversal that follows.
  - Index rebalancing on scheduled dates, forcing mechanical flows.
  - Dividend and ex-dividend dates.
  - Sector seasonality with a physical cause: natural gas demand in winter,
    retail in Q4, agricultural cycles.
  - Option expiry and quarterly triple-witching.

What these share is a MECHANISM you can state in one sentence without using the
word "cycle." That is the test.

A pattern is probably curve-fitting when:

  - The period was discovered by searching many periods and keeping the best.
    If you test 100 cycle lengths at the 5% level, five will look significant by
    construction. Almost nobody reports how many they tested.
  - The cycle length needs re-tuning every few months to keep working. A real
    period does not drift to fit the data.
  - It only works on one instrument and vanishes on similar ones.
  - It only works on the sample where it was found. Out-of-sample decay from
    "significant" to "nothing" is the norm for discovered cycles.
  - The explanation invokes market psychology in general terms rather than a
    specific, datable, repeating event.
  - Removing a handful of observations destroys it.

The asymmetry worth internalising: a real cycle needs a calendar or an
institutional forcing function. "Prices tend to oscillate" is not a mechanism —
it is a description of any mean-reverting noise process, including one you
generated with a random number generator.

Relevant to this project: the screener uses no cycle detection at all. RSI
appears only as a momentum band (50-70 good, >75 flagged), and the one-month
horizon is justified by post-upgrade analyst drift — a mechanism with a date
attached — not by any wave-fitting. That was a deliberate choice.


================================================================================
PART 2 — ETFs
================================================================================

2.1 STRUCTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
An ETF is a fund whose shares trade on an exchange. The mechanism that makes it
work is creation/redemption: Authorised Participants (large institutions) can
exchange a basket of the underlying securities for a block of ETF shares
("creation unit", typically 25,000-50,000 shares) and vice versa.

That mechanism is an arbitrage loop. If the ETF trades above the value of its
holdings (NAV), an AP buys the basket, creates shares, and sells them, pushing
the price back down. This is why ETF prices track NAV closely, and why they can
break: in a stressed market, if the underlying stops trading or becomes
impossible to price, the arbitrage loop stalls and the ETF can trade at a
meaningful discount or premium. That happened in corporate bond ETFs in March
2020.

Legal structures differ and it matters for taxes and mechanics:
  - Open-end funds (most equity ETFs) — can reinvest dividends, lend securities.
  - Unit Investment Trusts (SPY, QQQ historically) — cannot reinvest dividends,
    which creates a small cash drag.
  - Grantor trusts (commodity funds like GLD) — hold the physical asset.
  - ETNs — unsecured debt of the issuer, NOT a fund. You carry issuer credit
    risk. If the bank fails, your ETN is a claim in bankruptcy.


2.2 EXPENSE RATIOS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Quoted annually, accrued daily out of NAV. You never see a bill; the fund just
shrinks slightly relative to its index.

Typical ranges as of 2026:
  - Broad market index (VOO, VTI, SPY):        0.03% - 0.09%
  - Sector and factor funds:                   0.10% - 0.50%
  - Actively managed:                          0.35% - 1.00%
  - Leveraged / inverse:                       0.85% - 1.10%
  - Niche thematic:                            0.50% - 0.95%

For a one-month hold, the expense ratio is nearly irrelevant: 0.95% annual is
about 0.08% over a month, which is smaller than a typical bid-ask spread on a
thin fund. Expense ratios matter for years-long holds; spreads matter for
short ones. Optimising the wrong one is a common beginner error.


2.3 LIQUIDITY AND SPREADS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The counter-intuitive fact: an ETF's tradable liquidity is not its own volume.
It is the liquidity of what it holds, because APs can always create more shares
from the underlying basket.

A fund with 20,000 shares/day of screen volume that holds S&P 500 constituents
can absorb a large order at a fair price. A fund with the same screen volume
holding illiquid small-cap foreign equities cannot.

What actually drives the spread you pay:
  - Liquidity of the underlying basket (dominant factor).
  - Whether the underlying market is open. An ETF on Japanese equities trading
    at 2pm New York has a stale basket; market makers widen spreads to cover the
    risk, which is why international ETF spreads blow out outside local hours.
  - Volatility — spreads widen with uncertainty.
  - Time of day. The open and the close are the worst. The first and last 15
    minutes routinely carry spreads several times the midday level.
  - Competition among market makers.

Practical rules: use limit orders, never market orders, on anything but the most
liquid funds. Avoid the first and last 15 minutes. Compare the quote to the
fund's iNAV where published. For a fund quoting 0.30% spread, a round trip costs
0.60% before you are right about anything — on a one-month trade that is a
meaningful hurdle.


2.4 LEVERAGED AND INVERSE DECAY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the single most misunderstood product category in retail investing, so
here is the arithmetic.

A 3x ETF promises 3x the DAILY return, and it rebalances its exposure every day
to maintain that ratio. Compounding daily returns is not the same as multiplying
a period return by three.

Worked example. Index at 100, 3x fund at 100.
  Day 1: index +10% -> 110.   Fund +30% -> 130.
  Day 2: index -9.09% -> 100. Fund -27.27% -> 94.55.

The index is exactly flat. The fund lost 5.45%. Nobody made a mistake; no fee
caused it. It is the geometry of compounding, and it is called volatility decay
or beta slippage.

The loss scales roughly with the square of volatility and linearly with time:

    drag ≈ (L^2 - L) / 2 * sigma^2 * t

where L is leverage and sigma is volatility. At 3x leverage in a 40% volatility
environment, that is roughly 48% annualised drag against you in a flat market.

Consequences:
  - In a choppy sideways market, leveraged funds bleed in both directions.
    Both a 3x long and a 3x short on the same index can lose money in the same
    month. This is normal, not a malfunction.
  - In a smooth sustained trend they can OUTPERFORM their multiple, because
    daily rebalancing compounds gains.
  - Holding periods should be days, not months. The prospectuses say this
    explicitly and are ignored.
  - Inverse funds have the same problem plus a structural headwind, since
    equities drift up over long horizons.

For the one-month horizon this screener targets, leveraged ETFs are a poor fit.


2.5 HOW ETFs DIFFER FROM SINGLE STOCKS FOR SHORT-TERM TRADING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  - No idiosyncratic catalyst. A diversified ETF cannot gap 30% on a trial
    result or a buyout. The fat right tail that makes single-stock momentum
    attractive is diversified away, along with the fat left tail.
  - No analyst coverage. Nobody issues a price target on SPY. This is why the
    screener's entire selection mechanism — consensus ratings, upgrades,
    target upside — is inapplicable to ETFs. The screener found roughly 2,500
    NYSEARCA and BATS listings in the 18,337-stock universe, and essentially
    none of them can pass an analyst-based filter, because the data does not
    exist.
  - Earnings dates do not apply, though the underlying holdings' earnings do
    move the fund.
  - Lower volatility at equal notional, from diversification.
  - Tracking error and roll costs in commodity and volatility products can
    dominate the thesis. VIX futures ETFs in contango lose to roll regardless
    of direction.
  - Tax treatment varies by structure (commodities, currencies, and ETNs all
    differ from equity funds).

Implication for this project: ETFs need a completely separate scoring model
based on flows, momentum, spread, and tracking quality. Scoring them with the
current analyst-driven model would produce nonsense, which is exactly why
`explore` treats them as their own category and ranks them on price/technical
signals only.

PART 3 — PENNY STOCKS
================================================================================

3.1 OTC VERSUS LISTED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
"Penny stock" is about price (SEC: under $5), not venue. The venue is a separate
and more important axis.

LISTED (NYSE, NASDAQ, NYSE American):
  - Must meet initial and continued listing standards: minimum price, market
    cap, shareholder count, corporate governance.
  - Full SEC reporting.
  - Sub-$1 for 30 consecutive days triggers a deficiency notice; failure to cure
    means delisting to OTC. This is why you see reverse splits — they are often
    a listing-compliance manoeuvre, not a vote of confidence.

OTC — tiered, and the tier tells you most of what you need:
  - OTCQX     — highest tier, audited financials, no shells, no sub-penny.
  - OTCQB     — venture tier, $0.01 minimum bid, annual verification.
  - Pink      — subdivided by disclosure: Current, Limited, or No Information.
                "Pink No Information" means the company tells you nothing.
  - Expert Market — broker-dealers cannot publicly quote these; retail
                effectively cannot buy. Often where failed companies end up.

In the screener's universe, OTCMKTS was the single largest exchange bucket
(~2,500 listings). Almost none of it is investable by the criteria in this
project.


3.2 FLOAT AND DILUTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Float = shares actually available to trade, i.e. shares outstanding minus
insider and restricted holdings. Small float means violent moves on modest
volume, in both directions.

The structural problem with the sub-$1 world is dilution, and the common
mechanism is the toxic convertible:

  - Company needs cash and cannot raise conventionally.
  - It issues a convertible note to a specialist financier.
  - The note converts at a DISCOUNT to market price — often 20-40% below
    whatever the price is at conversion time.
  - The financier converts, immediately sells into the market, price falls.
  - The lower price means the next conversion yields MORE shares.
  - Repeat.

This is a death spiral, and it is self-reinforcing by design. The share count
can go from 50 million to several billion inside a year. Every technical signal
on the chart is noise next to a financing structure that mechanically issues
shares into any rally.

How to check before believing any chart: read the share count trend across the
last several 10-Qs. If shares outstanding are compounding, nothing else matters.
Also check for a recent reverse split — a 1-for-100 reverse split followed by a
price collapse back to the old level is the signature of a company that has done
this repeatedly.


3.3 LIQUIDITY TRAPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The recurring pattern: entering is easy, exiting is not.

  - Spreads. A stock quoted 0.0021 x 0.0025 carries a ~19% spread. You are
    down 19% the instant you fill, and you need a 23% move just to break even.
  - Displayed size is tiny. The bid might be 5,000 shares. Your 500,000-share
    position cannot exit at the quote; you walk the book down.
  - Market orders are dangerous — you can fill far from the last print.
  - Halts. The SEC can suspend trading for two weeks on public-interest
    grounds, usually after promotion. You cannot exit a halted stock at any
    price, and reopening prints are frequently catastrophic.
  - Pump-and-dump. Newsletter and social promotion, price spikes on volume,
    promoters exit into retail buying, price round-trips. The volume that
    attracts a screener is the same volume created by the promotion.
  - Sub-penny tick behaviour makes a "1 cent move" a 50% move.

The screener's $1,000,000 average-daily-dollar-volume filter exists exactly to
exclude this. That single threshold removes most of the OTC universe. The
`explore` penny-stock mode deliberately relaxes the price floor but keeps a
volume floor, and labels the result as higher risk, because the alternative is
producing a list of untradeable names.


3.4 WHY ANALYST COVERAGE IS NEARLY ABSENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The screener's own data makes this concrete: of 18,337 stocks pulled,
approximately 11,324 had NO consensus rating at all. That is about 62% of the
universe with zero analyst coverage.

The economics explain it entirely:

  - Research is expensive. An analyst covering a name properly costs a firm
    low-to-mid six figures a year, all-in.
  - Research is paid for indirectly — through trading commissions, investment
    banking relationships, or (post-MiFID II in Europe) explicit payment.
  - A company with a $20 million market cap cannot generate enough institutional
    commission flow to fund coverage. Institutions cannot buy a meaningful
    position without moving the price, so they do not participate, so there is
    no commission.
  - No banking fees either — these companies do not do large secondaries or
    acquisitions through bulge-bracket banks.
  - Compliance risk. Covering a company with thin disclosure and unreliable
    financials creates liability for the research firm.
  - Index exclusion. Not being in an index means no passive flow and no
    benchmark-driven institutional interest.

Where "coverage" of microcaps does exist, it is frequently ISSUER-PAID research:
the company pays a firm to publish. Disclosure is legally required but buried.
Treat a lone "Strong Buy" on a $30 million company as marketing until proven
otherwise.

This is the honest reason the screener's core method cannot be pointed at penny
stocks. The entire scoring model rests on analyst consensus, upgrade drift, and
price targets. In a universe with no analysts, there is nothing to score. The
`explore` penny mode therefore falls back to price and volume signals only, and
says so.

PART 4 — HOW THIS CONNECTS TO THE SCREENER
================================================================================

  - RSI 50-70 scoring (score.py: momentum_score) is the only oscillator concept
    used. No cycle detection, no wave fitting, by choice.
  - The one-month horizon is justified by analyst-upgrade drift, which has a
    date-stamped mechanism, not by any cyclical claim.
  - The $1M dollar-volume floor and the 3-analyst minimum are what keep the
    main screen out of the penny-stock and no-coverage swamp.
  - ETFs and penny stocks are separated into their own `explore` categories
    precisely because the analyst-based model does not transfer to them, and
    pretending otherwise would generate confident nonsense.

PART 5 - THE SECOND OPINION: CHECKING THE PICKS ON MARKETBEAT
Added 2026-10-06
================================================================================

5.1 WHY A SECOND SOURCE AT ALL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The screener picks stocks from Yahoo Finance. Every filter it applies - twenty
or more buy ratings, three or fewer holds, no sells, a consensus target above
the current price - is applied to numbers that arrive from a single undocumented
endpoint, with no contract, no support, and no guarantee that the figure is even
self-consistent. If that endpoint is wrong, or if a parser misreads it, the
screener does not fail loudly. It produces a confident, well-formatted list of
stocks that nobody checked.

This has already happened twice in this project. Relative volume was read as a
ratio when it is published as a percentage, so a stock trading 27% BELOW its
average volume was scored as though it were trading 72 times heavier than
normal. Free cash flow was labelled in dollars when the figure was in New Taiwan
dollars, turning US$23B into a reported $730B. Both were wrong by a wide margin,
both looked entirely plausible on the dashboard, and both were found by accident
rather than by a check.

So the last stage of the pipeline does not look for new stocks. It takes the
stocks already chosen and asks a second, independent organisation the same
questions. MarketBeat compiles analyst ratings itself, from its own providers -
Benzinga among them - so its buy/hold/sell count for a stock is an independent
count, not a copy of Yahoo's. Where the two agree, the number is probably real.
Where they disagree, one of them is wrong, and a stock should not be bought on
the strength of a line that two sources cannot confirm.

Note what this can and cannot do. It cannot make a stock good; nothing in this
stage can add a name to the list. It can only reject one. That asymmetry is
deliberate: a confirmation stage that can promote stocks becomes a second
screener with half the data behind it.


5.2 THE THIRTEEN CHECKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Each pick's MarketBeat pages are read - the overview, forecast, earnings,
short-interest, insider-trades, institutional-ownership, options and news tabs -
and each of the following becomes a check that passes, warns, or fails.

  1   Analyst forecast      MarketBeat's own buy/hold/sell ratio. Judged as a
                            proportion, not matched against Yahoo's count: the
                            two poll different brokers, so a stray sell is
                            normal. Fails under 60% buys, on a bearish
                            consensus word, on two or more sells amounting to
                            over a tenth of coverage, or on a quarter of the
                            coverage saying sell. 60-80% buys warns.
  2   Coverage              eight or more analysts passes; three to seven warns;
                            fewer fails. A consensus of two is not a consensus.
  3   Consensus trend       buy ratings now against buy ratings a month ago.
                            Decaying coverage warns even when the level is high.
  4   Price target          upside to MarketBeat's consensus target. Negative
                            fails outright; under 5% warns.
  5   Recent actions        upgrades, downgrades, target raises and cuts in the
                            last thirty days. More downgrades than upgrades
                            fails.
  6   MarketRank            MarketBeat's own percentile and its analyst
                            sub-score out of five.
  7   Earnings record       beat rate over the last four quarters, plus the
                            average surprise. Under 75% warns.
  8   Earnings event        a report landing inside the holding window warns:
                            a one-month position through an earnings date is a
                            different bet than the screener scored.
  9   Short interest        over 20% of float fails, 10-20% warns.
 10   Insider activity      ninety-day buy and sell dollar values.
 11   Institutional flow    funds adding against funds trimming.
 12   Options               whether the position can be hedged at all.
 13   Fact check            see 5.3. This is the one that answers "is the data
                            true", and the only check that can fail a stock on
                            grounds that have nothing to do with the company.

Any failure rejects the stock. Two or more warnings make it mixed. Everything
else is confirmed. The verdict is reported with the percentage of checks that
came back clean, so "confirmed on 11 of 13" and "confirmed on 6 of 13, the rest
unavailable" do not read the same.


5.3 THE FACT CHECK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Twelve numbers are put side by side - share price, consensus target, upside,
analyst count, market cap, P/E, PEG, short percentage of float, institutional
and insider ownership, dividend yield, beta - plus the buy/hold/sell breakdown
as a thirteenth row. Each has a tolerance, because two organisations computing
the same quantity from different filings on different days will never match to
the cent.

Three details matter more than the list itself.

Proportions, not counts, for the ratings. Comparing 54/1/0 against 58/2/1 as raw
numbers makes them look like a contradiction; they are the same consensus
measured over slightly different analyst sets. The comparison is on the share of
buys and the share of sells, with fifteen and ten percentage points of slack.
What it is built to catch is the real failure: one source showing an
overwhelming buy consensus while the other shows a hold.

Staleness is not disagreement. A stored price from last week will not match
today's quote, and that says nothing about whether the data was ever true. When
the stored row predates the check, price and upside are marked stale rather than
disputed, and they cannot fail the stock.

Only three fields can reject on their own: the ratings breakdown, the consensus
target, and the price. Those are the figures the pick was actually made on. PEG
disagreeing is worth knowing - on NVDA the two sources report 0.29 and 1.81,
which is a six-fold difference arising from different growth assumptions - but
it is a methodology difference, not evidence that either page is lying, so it
warns.

Measured on the first real run, NVDA matched on twelve of thirteen figures and
Energy Transfer on twelve of thirteen. The disagreements were PEG and insider
ownership percentage respectively. Neither stock was rejected on the data; both
came back mixed on company grounds - heavy insider selling and net fund
trimming for NVDA, a beat rate of one quarter in four for Energy Transfer.

One bug this stage found in its own first run is worth recording, because it is
the exact failure the stage exists to prevent. The URL for each stock was taken
from the stored universe row, which in the Yahoo era holds a Yahoo Finance
address. The "MarketBeat check" was therefore re-reading Yahoo pages, parsing
them with MarketBeat's parsers, and reporting agreement with itself. It now
builds marketbeat.com addresses from the exchange code and verifies the page
exists before reading it. A verification stage that silently verifies against
the source it is meant to be independent of is worse than no verification at
all, because it produces confidence rather than doubt.


5.4 IS THERE A TRUSTED MARKETBEAT API
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
No. This was checked properly rather than assumed.

MarketBeat publishes no developer API and no data feed. Its FAQ covers accounts,
subscriptions, newsletters, alerts, data sources and CSV export for All-Access
members, and says nothing about programmatic access; there is no developer
portal, no API documentation and no published endpoint. The only sanctioned
machine-readable route out of an All-Access account is the Excel/CSV export
button on the screener and list pages. Anything else - including what this
project does - is reading the HTML of pages the account is entitled to see.

That is worth being clear-eyed about. Scraping a logged-in session is not an
API: the markup can change without notice, there is no rate-limit contract, and
the terms of service govern what the data may be used for. For a private tool
making private decisions this is a reasonable risk. It would not be a reasonable
foundation for anything redistributed.

The ratings themselves do have a real API, one layer upstream. MarketBeat's
analyst-ratings data comes from Benzinga and other providers, and Benzinga sells
a documented commercial API: an analyst-ratings endpoint covering US equities
with history back to 2013, JSON or XML, intraday delivery, ratings expressed in
the analyst's own language rather than normalised. Overnight changes publish
roughly three hours before the US open. It is key-authenticated and paid -
pricing is quote-only, not listed - and it is the only properly trusted,
supported source of this specific data at a reachable price point.

The practical conclusion for this project: keep MarketBeat as the second opinion
through the logged-in browser, because the subscription is already paid for and
the stage reads only a handful of stocks per run. If the project ever needs
ratings data it can depend on - as a primary source, on a schedule, or for
anything shared with anyone else - the answer is a Benzinga key, not a better
scraper.

5.5 THE FIRST VERSION WAS TOO STRICT, AND WHY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The check originally rejected any stock with a single sell rating on MarketBeat,
mirroring the screener's own zero-sell filter. Run against a real shortlist it
rejected almost everything: Microsoft at 41 buy / 5 hold / 2 sell and Synopsys
at 18 / 3 / 1 both failed, as did eight of eleven stocks in a menu run.

The mistake was treating MarketBeat as a copy of Yahoo that had to agree analyst
for analyst. It is not. The two poll different sets of brokers, so one or two
sells out of fifty is the normal state of affairs, not a contradiction. What
MarketBeat is actually being asked is whether *its own* ratio says the stock
goes up.

So the rule is now proportional. 85% buys with two sells out of forty-eight is a
buy-weighted ratio and warns; 36% buys with a Hold consensus is not and fails.
On the same eleven stocks that produced ten rejections, the proportional rule
gives five mixed and six rejected - and the six are rejected for reasons that
mean something: Frontline at 36% buys and Nordic American at 25% with Hold
consensuses, SRZN and ABSI with 27.7% and 24.3% of their float sold short, PICS
on a fresh downgrade.

One detail matters for thinly covered stocks. A lone sell among nine analysts is
11% of coverage, which would trip a flat ten-percent rule on arithmetic alone.
Failing therefore needs either two sells above that tenth, or a quarter of the
coverage however thin it is.

The same lesson applies to the figures. Requiring every number to match made
ordinary methodology differences look like lies - PEG at 0.29 against 1.81 is
two different growth assumptions, not a false page. A disagreement now fails a
stock only when it would have changed the answer: a price more than 10% apart,
a target below the price where ours was above it, or a consensus pointing down
where ours pointed up.

5.6 HOW TO RUN IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    python run.py login                  once, to save the All-Access session
    python run.py verify                 check today's ranked picks
    python run.py verify --verify-top 20 check more of them
    python run.py verify --headless      no visible Chrome window
    python run.py all --no-verify        skip the stage in a full run

From the menu, M on the results screen checks the stocks on screen and shows
each verdict as it lands. Verdicts are stored in the verify table, keyed by
date, alongside the full MarketBeat read they were based on.

Eight page loads per stock at half a second apiece puts one stock at roughly ten
seconds, so the default of ten picks takes under two minutes. Logged out it
still works, but the paywalled columns come back blank and fewer checks can run.


================================================================================
PART 6 - THE THIRD SOURCE: NASDAQ, AND THE REST OF THE API SURVEY
Added 2026-10-06
================================================================================

6.1 WHAT WAS LOOKED FOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Part 5 established that MarketBeat has no API and that the ratings underneath it
come from Benzinga, which does sell one. That left an open question: is there a
source of analyst consensus that is free, needs no login, and can be called like
an API rather than scraped?

Five candidates were tested from this machine rather than read about.

  nasdaq.com backend    HTTP 200, no key, real data      <- works
  Alpha Vantage         HTTP 200 with the demo key       <- works, documented
  Finnhub               HTTP 401 "Please use an API key" <- free key available
  Financial Modeling    HTTP 401 "Invalid API KEY"       <- free key available
  stockanalysis.com     HTTP 404 on the guessed path     <- not pursued

6.2 THE NASDAQ BACKEND
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
nasdaq.com serves its own stock pages from a JSON backend at api.nasdaq.com that
answers without a key, a token or a login. Six endpoints were confirmed working:

  analyst/{sym}/targetprice        consensus buy/hold/sell, low/mean/high target,
                                   and a twelve-month history of the consensus
  analyst/{sym}/ratings            mean rating, analyst count, broker names
  quote/{sym}/short-interest       the semi-monthly reports, days to cover
  company/{sym}/institutional-     institutional ownership %, holders that
    holdings                       increased and decreased positions
  company/{sym}/insider-trades     open-market buys and sells, 3 and 12 months
  company/{sym}/earnings-surprise  reported EPS against consensus, by quarter

That is most of what the eight MarketBeat tabs give, from a different compiler,
for nothing. For NVDA on 2026-10-06 it returned 32 buys / 0 holds / 0 sells from
39 analysts, a mean target of 325.03 against a last price of 239.42, short
interest of 294,225,803 shares at 2.55 days to cover, 79.53% institutional
ownership with 3,374 holders adding and 2,575 trimming, 2 insider buys against
17 sells in three months, and four consecutive earnings beats averaging +7.4%.

Three limits are worth writing down plainly.

It is not a documented public API. It is the site's own backend: no published
contract, no versioning promise, no rate-limit statement. Eight sequential calls
took 26 seconds with no block, which is about 3.3 seconds a call - slow enough
that the client fetches a stock's seven endpoints in parallel, making a stock
take about as long as its slowest call instead of seven times as long.

Coverage is patchy. NVDA, SOFI and TSM return a consensus; ET and BRK.B return
an empty one. A stock Nasdaq does not cover is reported as unknown and warns,
never as a failure - absence of data is not evidence against a stock.

It publishes shares short but not the percentage of float, so the percentage is
computed from shares outstanding. Outstanding is larger than float, so the
figure runs slightly low: 1.22% for NVDA against Yahoo's 1.27% and MarketBeat's
1.30%. Close enough to cross-check, not close enough to quote.

6.3 THE DOCUMENTED ALTERNATIVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Alpha Vantage's OVERVIEW endpoint returns AnalystRatingStrongBuy, Buy, Hold,
Sell and StrongSell plus AnalystTargetPrice - the exact five-bucket breakdown
this project filters on - and it is a real documented API with a free key. For
IBM through the public demo key it returned 3 strong buy, 10 buy, 10 hold, 1
sell, 1 strong sell and a target of 240.59. The catch is the free tier: 25
requests a day and 5 a minute. That is enough to verify a ten-stock shortlist
once a day and nothing more. Paid tiers start at $49.99/month for 75 requests a
minute.

Finnhub has /stock/recommendation (the buy/hold/sell breakdown by month) on its
free tier at roughly 60 calls a minute, non-commercial use only; /stock/price-
target is premium. A free key would cover the ratings half of a cross-check at a
much higher call budget than Alpha Vantage allows.

Financial Modeling Prep has /stable/grades and a price-target-consensus
endpoint. Both need a key; a free tier exists with limits.

Polygon (now Massive) resells Benzinga's ratings through its own REST API, with
individual plans at $0, $29, $79 and $199 a month, the Benzinga datasets sitting
on the paid tiers. This is the cheapest properly-licensed route to the data that
MarketBeat itself is built on, and the one to take if ratings ever need to be
dependable rather than merely checked.

6.4 WHAT WAS ACTUALLY BUILT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Nasdaq, as a third source inside the verification stage, in screener/nasdaq.py.
MarketBeat still carries the company judgement - it has MarketRank, news
sentiment, the full upgrade history and the options tab, none of which Nasdaq
exposes - and Nasdaq now adds two things to every stock:

  - its own consensus check: buy/hold/sell counts and target upside from the
    brokers reporting to the exchange, which fails a stock if more than a sixth
    of them rate it a sell and warns if fewer than 60% say buy
  - a second vote on every figure in the fact check

That second vote changes what the fact check means. With one other source, a
figure either matched or it did not, and a single methodology difference looked
like a contradiction. With two, each row is confirmed (at least one source
agrees), disputed (every available source disagrees), or unconfirmed (nobody
else publishes it). Only a disputed core figure - the ratings breakdown, the
consensus target, or the price - fails the stock.

Measured on NVDA and Energy Transfer, 2026-10-06:

  NVDA    MIXED, 71% of checks clean
          12 of 13 figures confirmed. Price agreed to the cent across all three
          (239.24 / 239.24 / 239.42), targets within 7 dollars of each other,
          and the ratings agreed as proportions despite the raw counts reading
          58/2/1, 54/1/0 and 32/0/0. Only PEG was disputed, at 0.29 against
          MarketBeat's 1.81 - a growth-assumption difference, not a lie.
          Mixed on company grounds: $399M of insider selling, more funds
          trimming than adding, a target cut inside the month.

  ET      MIXED, 79% of checks clean
          12 of 13 confirmed by MarketBeat. Nasdaq publishes no consensus for
          it, which warns rather than failing. Insider ownership disputed,
          10.29% against 3.28%. Mixed on company grounds: it beat estimates in
          one quarter of the last four.

Neither was rejected, and the one real data disagreement on each stock was
visible with both readings printed beside it. That is the whole point: a figure
nobody can confirm should be looked at rather than traded on.

6.5 RUNNING IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Nothing extra is needed - no key, no account. The Nasdaq calls happen alongside
the MarketBeat read in the same stage.

    python run.py verify                 both sources, today's ranked picks
    python run.py verify --headless       no visible Chrome window

From the menu, M on the results screen checks the stocks on screen; each row
shows the verdict, how many figures were confirmed, and which sources confirmed
them. O opens the MarketBeat page for the selected stock, N opens the Nasdaq
page.

A stock costs about eight MarketBeat page loads and seven Nasdaq calls, the
Nasdaq ones in parallel, which comes to roughly fifteen seconds a stock.


================================================================================
PART 7 - DOES ANY OF THIS WORK? BACKTESTS, A WEEK OF REAL PICKS, AND TIME TRAVEL
Added 2026-10-15
================================================================================

7.1 THE QUESTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything up to Part 6 is about whether the screener reads its data correctly.
None of it says whether the stocks it picks go up. This part tests that three
ways, from the most direct to the most thorough, and reports what came back
including the parts that are unflattering.


7.2 A WEEK OF REAL PICKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The run of 2026-10-06 produced fifteen ranked stocks. One week later (Oct 13):

  5 of 15 rose. The average pick fell 2.20% while the S&P 500 was flat (-0.14%).
  By Oct 15 the average pick was down 5.09% against the S&P's -0.94%.
  The two stocks the verification stage CONFIRMED, AVGO and TSM, fell the most:
  -6.32% for the week and -10.41% to date.

The weekly price ranges were too narrow. Only 8 of the 15 closes landed inside
the 68% range (about 10 should have) and 13 of 15 inside the 95% range (about
14). The ranges were built from 20-day volatility measured during a calm stretch.

The single biggest cause of the loss was concentration. CLS, AVGO, ANET, TSM and
SNPS - five of the top eight - are all chip and AI-hardware companies, and they
fell together. Scoring each stock on its own never noticed.

One correction worth recording. The large percentages shown beside each stock,
like AVGO's +41%, were analysts' 12-month price targets. The screener's
one-week figure was always a range centred on no change. A one-week test can
check the range, not the target.


7.3 THE BACKTEST: DOES THE CONSENSUS PREDICT THE NEXT MONTH?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Nasdaq's analyst endpoint returns the consensus for each of the last twelve
months, which is the one piece of history this test needs and neither
MarketBeat nor Yahoo will give out. 400 random liquid stocks were drawn; 176 had
usable history, for 2,043 stock-months from 2025-10 to 2026-09. Each month's
buy/hold/sell count was paired with what the stock did next.

Two precautions. Returns are measured against the average sampled stock in the
same month, so a rising market is not credited to the ratings. And significance
is judged across months rather than stock-months: stocks move together, so two
thousand observations from twelve months behave more like twelve.

                                    next week          next month
                                  vs avg     t       vs avg     t
  95%+ of analysts say buy        +0.78%   2.2       +1.80%   1.6
  passes the verification line    +0.40%   2.3       +0.62%   1.4
  buy ratings rising              +0.19%   0.3       +0.08%  -0.1
  zero sells                      +0.03%   0.4       +0.05%   0.5
  the brief: 20+ / <=3 / 0        -0.50%  -0.7       -2.37%  -2.0   (29 cases)
  rank correlation, buy share -> next month's return: +0.012 (t=0.4)

A t under about 2 means the result could easily be chance, and with 24 groups
tested about one would cross 2 by luck alone. Four did, and they do not agree
across horizons. The honest reading: at most a faint edge at the very top of
the buy-share scale over a week, gone by a month, and no sign that the 20/3/0
shape works.


7.4 TIME TRAVEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
`python run.py timetravel` goes back to the first trading day of each of the
last twelve months and rebuilds what the screener could have known that day:
Nasdaq's consensus as it stood, and every price measure computed from closes up
to that day and none after. It then runs the live scoring function on that data
and holds the top ten for a month. The rule sets differ only in the fixes, so
each one's effect shows on its own.

What cannot be replayed is held equal for every stock so it cannot tilt the
ranking: upgrades, price targets, MarketBeat's features, beta, earnings dates
and insider trades. Nobody publishes their history. The replay therefore tests
the consensus and the price signals - the part that can be reconstructed
honestly.

1,200 stocks drawn, 483 with enough history, 12 months:

  S&P 500 over the year +11.9%, the average sampled stock +18.0%

  rules              1 month   vs avg     t    beat avg   grew to
  old (20/3/0)        -0.73%   -2.18%  -1.9     5/12      -9.1%   (3.4 picks a month)
  old, scaled 12/2/0  +0.13%   -1.31%  -1.4     3/12      +0.9%
  + sector cap        -0.03%   -1.47%  -1.4     4/12      -1.2%
  + 95% buys          +6.52%   +5.07%   1.8     8/12    +102.4%
  new (95% + cap)     +5.80%   +4.35%   1.5     8/12     +86.7%

The 20/3/0 rule as written found only 3.4 stocks a month, because Nasdaq counts
fewer analysts than Yahoo - 27 against 47 on AVGO - so twenty buys on Nasdaq is
a much stricter cut. The 12/2/0 version scales it to Nasdaq's counts.

The +102% needed checking before it could be believed, and it does not survive.

It is not one lucky pick. ERAS rose 195% in January 2026, but without every move
over 50% the rule still averaged +4.9% a month, and the median pick +2.0%
against the old rules' -0.02%.

It is mostly a size tilt. Asking for 95% of analysts to say buy is easy to meet
with five analysts and hard with forty, so the rule fills up with thinly covered
smaller companies - ECPG, AROC, ENVA, LGND - while the old rules held MSFT, KO,
GOOG and V. In this particular year, fewer analysts meant higher returns
whatever the rating: stocks with 3-5 analysts averaged +2.41% a month, with 21+
only +0.47%.

And survivorship bias inflates exactly that tilt. The sample is drawn from
companies worth $2B or more today. A small company that soared during the year
grew into the sample; one that collapsed fell out of it.

The fair test compares each 95%-buy stock with others followed by the same
number of analysts:

  analysts    95%+ buy vs same-coverage peers     t
  3-5              +2.91% a month                1.5
  6-10             +5.03%                        1.6
  11-20            -3.99%                       -1.6

Not significant anywhere, and the sign flips. That is noise, not a signal, and
the screener keeps the 20/3/0 rule. Switching would have turned it into a
small-cap bet with a backtest that flatters it.


7.4b THE REPLICATION - AND WHY IT MATTERS MORE THAN THE FIRST RESULT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The replay was run a second time after a fresh pipeline run. The same random
seed drew from a refreshed universe, so the stocks were different - and the
ranking of the rules turned over completely:

  rules                first sample     second sample
  old (20/3/0)            -9.1%            +73.7%   (2.9 picks a month, 66% one sector)
  old, scaled 12/2/0      +0.9%             -8.5%
  + sector cap            -1.2%             -8.0%
  + 95% buys            +102.4%            +24.3%
  new (95% + cap)        +86.7%            +23.9%

Against same-coverage peers in the second sample: old +3.15% (t=1.2), scaled
-1.67% (t=-1.5), 95% buys +0.27% (t=0.3). Nothing significant, again.

A rule with a real edge wins on both samples. Here the winner depends on which
stocks were drawn, which is what noise looks like. The +102% was never a
finding; it was one draw. Neither was the +73.7%, which came from fewer than
three stocks a month crowded two-thirds into one sector.

Two results did replicate, and those are the ones the screener now acts on:

  - One-year volatility gave the best-calibrated ranges both times, off by 2.2
    and then 2.4 points against the 20-day measure's 8.1 and 7.4.
  - The sector cap changed returns by a fraction of a point either way in both
    samples (-1.2% vs +0.9%, then -8.0% vs -8.5%). It costs almost nothing, and
    on the real 2026-10-06 list it would have halved that week's loss.

Everything about which consensus rule picks better stays unproven.


7.5 WHAT WAS CHANGED, AND WHAT IT DID
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Sector cap - at most three picks per sector, in the dashboard ranking and the
menu. It needed a fix of its own first: Yahoo's screener has never returned
sectors (0 of 22,786 stocks), so sector and industry now come from Yahoo's
assetProfile, inside the analyst request already being made. Stocks with no
known sector are never capped together. On the real 2026-10-06 list the cap
drops SNPS, AVGO and ANET for PR, BAC and LNG, and that week's top ten would
have fallen 2.10% instead of 4.57%. In the twelve-month replay it cost 0.2 to
0.7 points a month - concentration in winners helped in a rising year. It stays,
as risk control rather than a return booster.

Volatility for the price ranges - switched from 20-day to one-year. Over 5,737
stock-weeks, the share of next-week closes inside each band (targets 68.3% and
95.4%):

  20d     65.0% / 90.6%    the original: too narrow, worst in the tails
  1y      68.9% / 93.8%    closest on both - now the default
  max     72.3% / 95.1%
  blend   67.5% / 93.3%

Consensus rule - a switch was added ("share", 95% of analysts saying buy) and
left off, for the reasons in 7.4.

Odds table - calibrate.py drew its sample as the first 300 tickers in
alphabetical order, so the "measured odds" in the menu came almost entirely
from stocks beginning with A. It now uses the same seeded random sample as the
backtest.


7.6 THE CONCLUSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The price ranges are now close to honest: about 69% of outcomes inside a 68%
band. The stock selection is not shown to beat the market by any test here. The
analyst consensus carries, at most, a faint short-lived edge that disappears
once company size is controlled for.

That makes the screener good at something real and unremarkable: narrowing
22,000 listings to a shortlist of liquid, well-covered, analyst-favoured names
with their risks laid out and cross-checked against two other sources. It does
not make the shortlist a prediction. Anything bought from it should be sized as
though the edge were zero, because on this evidence it nearly is.

Every number in this part can be regenerated:

    python run.py backtest   --backtest-sample 400
    python run.py timetravel --backtest-sample 1200

with --seed for a different random sample. Observations, monthly results and
every replayed pick are written to data/.

PART 8 - MARKETBEAT ONLY: A DATABASE THAT REMEMBERS, A BACKTEST, A PLANNER
Added 2026-10-25
================================================================================

8.1 WHAT CHANGED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Yahoo Finance is gone: the code that read it, its imports, its settings and the
yfinance requirement. MarketBeat is now the only source of every figure -
candidates, buy/hold/sell counts, price targets, rating history and daily
prices. Parts 1 to 7 describe the Yahoo-era pipeline and are left as they were,
as the record of how the project got here.

Candidates come from about seventy of MarketBeat's research lists (upgrades, top
rated, breakouts, every sector). For each one the program reads three MarketBeat
pages: the overview (company, sector, price, volume), the chart (a year of daily
closes) and the forecast (the count, the target, MarketBeat's own counts from 1
month, 3 months and 1 year ago, and every analyst action listed).

The first run read 2,364 stocks in 15 minutes and stored 557,124 daily closes. A
second run the same day took 1.8 minutes: stocks already read today and price
history already current were not fetched again.


8.2 THE DATABASE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Everything goes into data/screener.db (SQLite, created on first run, schema in
screener/db.py). Two kinds of table:

  working   today's run only - the ranked list, scores, the dashboard's inputs
  history   never cleared - prices, rating snapshots, rating actions, runs,
            recommendations, backtests and their trades, holdings, plans

Earlier runs deleted the whole database first. That is why nothing could be
backtested from it: every run threw the previous one away.


8.3 THE PAST, REBUILT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A backtest has to know what MarketBeat showed on a past day. Its free pages
publish only four dated counts per stock (now, 1 month, 3 months, 1 year ago),
so the others are rebuilt from the analyst actions:

  1. each firm's latest rating on or before the day, if under a year old;
  2. for firms whose older actions have scrolled off the page, the rating is
     read backwards from their next action - an upgrade names the old rating,
     a target change keeps it, an initiation means there was none;
  3. the result is anchored to MarketBeat's own published counts on either side.

Checked against those published counts, the rebuilt buy share lands within 5
points for 83% of stocks one month back and 71% three months back. That is good
enough to rank by, and not good enough to trust blindly; every backtest says how
many of its picks were made on rebuilt counts.


8.4 THREE BACKTESTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Strategy: hold the stocks with the best ratio score, re-check after the holding
period, keep the ones still near the top, replace the rest. Whole shares, no
commissions, valued at MarketBeat's closes.

  #   stocks              period                  hold  return  buy&hold   S&P  trades  win
  9   all 2,226 stored    2025-10-23 - 2026-10-23 10/21d +40.3%   +6.2%  +13.1%   29   66%
  10  latest top 20 (*)   2026-04-23 - 2026-10-23  5/10d  +8.0%  +18.6%   +7.3%   10   50%
  11  20 large caps       2026-07-23 - 2026-10-23  3/5d   +7.5%   +4.2%   +3.0%    3  100%
  (*) chosen by today's ratings: hindsight, flatters the strategy

Every result was audited: the money balances to the cent, the win rate and
drawdown recompute, every trade price is a stored close, and every pick had
rating data dated on or before the trade.

What looked wrong, and what it turned out to be:

  - The first version of #9 returned +75%. Two trades made 70% of it, because
    a stock replacing a sold one got all the freed cash (HBM took a quarter of
    the portfolio). New positions now get an equal share of the portfolio.
    It also held GOOG and GOOGL at once; one listing per company now.
  - Shifting #9's start by a week moved buy-and-hold from +6% to +58%. One
    stock (ELOX) showed a thousandfold gain - a 1-for-1000 reverse split
    MarketBeat's chart never adjusted for. 57 stocks have one-day moves of 3x
    or more; they are now left out and the backtest says so. Nothing under $1
    is bought.
  - #11 traded three times in three months: the ratio ranking of large caps
    barely changes week to week, so "re-check every 5 days" rarely trades,
    and its 100% win rate is three trades.
  - #9 still beats the S&P with its two best stocks removed (+21%), and with
    the start moved by up to a month (+30% to +40%). But rebuilt counts can
    credit a firm with a rating it had not yet issued, if its initiation
    scrolled off the page. Using only MarketBeat's own published counts - no
    rebuilding at all - the top 10 made +19.7% against the S&P's +12.2%, but
    all of it in one two-month stretch; the top 20 made +4.6% and the top 50
    +4.1%, both behind the market.


8.5 THE CONCLUSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Same as part 7. The buy/hold/sell ratio picks companies analysts agree on. On
this year's data its best case beat the market and its cleanest test did not,
which is what no edge looks like. The planner and the backtest are tools for
sizing and checking decisions, not evidence that the list predicts returns.

    python run.py replay --start 2025-10-23 --cash 10000 --picks 10 --hold 21
    python run.py backtests
    python run.py plan --cash 5000

Every backtest is saved in the database and listed by the menu (B, then H).



================================================================================
PART 9 - THE PRICE CHART: SHOWN BESIDE THE RATIO, TESTED AS A RULE
Added 2026-10-26
================================================================================

9.1 WHAT IS SHOWN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every list now shows each stock's 1-week, 1-month and 1-year price change - the
ranges on MarketBeat's own chart - beside its buy/hold/sell count. They are
worked out from the closes already stored, ending at the price MarketBeat showed
on the day's run, so they cost no extra requests. Under the list, the selected
stock's other overview-page facts: sector, P/E and PEG, projected earnings
growth, short interest, insider buying and selling, news mood, institutional
ownership. Those are today's readings only; MarketBeat keeps no history of
them, so they can be shown but not backtested.

9.2 SHOULD THE CHART CHANGE THE RANKING?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Price history is stored, so trend rules can be replayed. Four were tried, each
on top of the ratio ranking (top 10 or 20, re-checked every 10 or 21 days):

  ratio only     the current list
  up1m           skip stocks down over the last month
  up3m           skip stocks down over the last 3 months
  blend3m        score = 70% ratio + 30% rank of 3-month change

132 replays: 8 start dates from 2026-01-26 to 2026-05-04 run to 2026-10-23, and
three separate 3-month blocks, each with three settings.

                 average  beats ratio-only  beats S&P  avg max drawdown
  ratio only     +13.8%         -             23/27        -9.8%
  up1m            +5.2%        4/27            6/27       -11.6%
  up3m           +15.9%       19/27           22/27       -10.4%
  blend3m        +16.5%       19/27           24/27       -15.2%
  (the windows overlap - they all end on the same day - so they are not 27
  independent results)

Skipping last month's losers is clearly worse: stocks that fell for a month
tended to bounce back. The 3-month rules look better, and they survived the
cleanest test too - with rating counts taken only from MarketBeat's own dated
counts, no rebuilding, up3m beat ratio-only in 6 of 8 runs (+11.4% vs +6.7%).

But where the extra came from:

                    return   two best stocks          share of   without
                                                      the gain   those two
  ratio only, Jan    +10.7%   MU, V                     66%        +8.9%
  up3m, Jan          +15.3%   MU, RBRK                 111%        +5.5%
  blend3m, Jan       +17.8%   WDC, MU                  126%       -2.7%
  ratio only, Mar    +18.6%   RBRK, V                   56%       +19.7%
  up3m, Mar          +19.6%   MU, RBRK                 112%        +1.4%
  blend3m, Mar       +27.0%   MU, RBRK                  78%        +3.2%

The trend rules made all of their extra, and more, on two or three stocks that
rode this year's chip and AI run (MU went up about fourfold). Without them the
trend rules did worse than the plain ratio. They also won fewer trades (39-46%
against 55%) and fell further at their worst (blend3m -19.7% in backtest #17).

9.3 THE DECISION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The ranking stays on the ratio alone. The chart is shown beside it so it can be
read, and the trend rules are an option in the backtest (B, then T on the last
step; or `python run.py replay --trend up3m`) for checking again as history
builds. One year that rewarded chasing a few winners is not enough to make it
the default. Saved runs: #14 ratio only, #15 up1m, #16 up3m, #17 blend3m
(2026-01-26 to 2026-10-23, top 10, 21 days).

To undo every change from this part, copy the files in
data/backups/backup_pre_chart_2026-10-26/ back over the project.


PART 10 - NET BUYS: MOST BUYS, FEWEST HOLDS (2026-10-27)

The old list ranked by the buy share (Wilson lower bound), so 37/0/0 (V) beat
54/1/0 (NVDA). The new score is net buys = buys - 2 x holds - 5 x sells, which
puts the most analysts saying Buy first: NVDA 52, AMZN 51, GOOGL 44, V 37.

66 backtests (8 rolling starts to 2026-10-23 plus three 3-month blocks; top 10
and top 20, 21-day holds), nothing saved:

  ranking                 mean    beats old   beats S&P   win    w/o best 2
